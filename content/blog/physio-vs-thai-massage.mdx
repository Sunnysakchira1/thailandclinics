See a physiotherapist if you have an injury, pain that's getting worse or a recovery to manage after surgery: a licensed physio assesses the cause and gives you a treatment plan. Book a Thai massage for general muscle tightness when you're otherwise well, because it may ease muscle aches for a while but it isn't a diagnosis or a treatment plan.

## The difference at a glance

| | Physiotherapy | Thai massage |
|---|---|---|
| **Who does it** | A physiotherapist licensed by the Physical Therapy Council of Thailand | A massage therapist certified for spa or health massage, or a licensed Thai traditional medicine practitioner |
| **First visit** | An assessment of your history, movement and the likely cause | Often straight into treatment |
| **The goal** | Find the cause, reduce pain and rebuild strength and movement | Relax tight muscles and ease short-term aches |
| **What you leave with** | A plan, progress checks and exercises to do at home | Often nothing to do at home |

## Who is licensed to do what

**Physiotherapy** is a regulated health profession in Thailand. Physiotherapists must be registered and licensed by the Physical Therapy Council under the Physical Therapy Profession Act B.E. 2547 (2004), and World Physiotherapy reports that the licence is granted by national examination. You can ask any physio for their name and licence number.

**Thai massage** sits under different rules. Massage at [spas and massage shops](/bangkok/wellness-clinics/) is "massage for health", regulated under the Health Establishment Act B.E. 2559 (2016). The people who give it must hold an accredited certificate and register as service providers with the Department of Health Service Support, part of the Ministry of Public Health. Thai massage used as medical treatment is part of Thai traditional medicine. Its practitioners are licensed by the Thai Traditional Medical Council under the Thai Traditional Medical Professions Act B.E. 2556 (2013).

Neither route makes a massage therapist a physiotherapist. After surgery, your rehab should be led by a licensed physio following your surgeon's protocol.

## What massage can and can't do

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) says the evidence that massage helps low-back pain is weak, and that for shoulder pain the benefits were short-term only. That fits how most people use a Thai massage: it loosens you up, but the problem can come back if something underneath is driving it.

A physio's job is to find that underlying cause. Weak hip muscles behind knee pain is one example, and a desk set-up behind neck pain is another. A physio treats it with hands-on work, exercise and home exercises.

## When a Thai massage is a bad idea

Massage is generally safe, but not risk-free. NCCIH notes rare reports of serious side effects, such as a blood clot, nerve injury or bone fracture, often linked to vigorous deep tissue massage or to older people. Skip the massage and get checked first if any of these apply:

- **A fresh sprain or strain.** The NHS advises avoiding massage for the first couple of days after a sprain or strain.
- **A possible fracture.** If you heard a crack or the area changed shape, go to a hospital emergency department. If you can't put weight on it, see a doctor first.
- **A swollen, painful calf or leg.** It could be a blood clot. A 2020 case report in *BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth* describes a pregnant Thai woman who had a life-threatening pulmonary embolism after a leg massage at a traditional massage shop. The authors warn that leg massage in someone with a deep vein thrombosis can dislodge the clot.
- **Pregnancy.** Because pregnant women are at higher risk of an undetected clot, the same authors say traditional massage in pregnancy should be avoided unless that risk has been ruled out.
- **Numbness, tingling or weakness.** These need a doctor, not a massage.
- **Recent surgery.** Ask your surgeon first.

Thai massage often involves strong pressure and stretching. A 2015 article in *Ancient Science of Life* on Thai traditional massage warned that rough technique carries a risk of fracture, and that massage over the carotid area of the neck can cause an embolism. If something hurts sharply, say stop.

Yes. Get a physio assessment first, then ask whether massage fits your plan and which areas to avoid. ## What to check

- **Is it an injury or just tightness?** Injury, worsening pain or anything after surgery means physio first.
- **Is your physio licensed?** Ask for a name and a Physical Therapy Council licence number.
- **Will your insurer pay?** Check the wording. Some plans cover physio but exclude massage, even when it eases pain.
- **Does the massage feel wrong?** Sharp pain, numbness or dizziness means stop.

## FAQ

### Is Thai massage the same as physiotherapy?

No. Physiotherapists are licensed by the Physical Therapy Council of Thailand and assess, treat and rehabilitate injuries. Thai massage is given by therapists certified for health massage, or by licensed Thai traditional medicine practitioners. It's a different profession with different training.

### Is Thai massage good for back pain?

It may ease muscle tightness for a while, but NCCIH says the evidence that massage treats low-back pain is weak. If back pain keeps returning, gets worse or comes with numbness or weakness, see a physiotherapist or doctor.

### Can a Thai massage make an injury worse?

It can. The NHS advises avoiding massage for the first couple of days after a sprain or strain. Strong pressure on a fracture, a blood clot or a recent operation can cause harm. Get an injury checked before you book a massage.

### Does insurance cover Thai massage?

Usually not as physiotherapy. Allianz Care's benefit guide, for example, says physiotherapy cover doesn't include massage. Check your own policy wording before you book.
